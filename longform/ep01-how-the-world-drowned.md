## Chapter 1 — A century of increments [VO] (~2 min)

`[VISUAL: slow storm footage; coastline stills; maps fading]`

The water did not rise all at once.

Each storm season reached a little further inland. Each harvest fed a few fewer people. Nobody woke up one morning to find the sea in their kitchen. Instead, a century passed in increments: a flood defence that was a few years overdue, a coastal road that was not worth repairing this time, a town that quietly stopped appearing in the budget.

Coastlines were redrawn, and then redrawn again, until anyone under forty knew the old maps only as pictures of places they would never see.

Even the word "unprecedented" wore out. The same disaster kept happening, year after year, and the language of emergency simply stopped working. You cannot keep declaring a crisis that never ends. Eventually it becomes the weather.

Governments defended the coast where they could afford to. Everywhere else, abandonment arrived without anyone announcing it. There was no proclamation, no flag lowered. Just missed repairs, and budgets that never quite accounted for the next storm. Within two generations, most of the old American Atlantic seaboard had disappeared from the planning documents.

That, more than the water, is what I find frightening. Whole regions did not fall. They were deprioritised.

## Chapter 2 — The week New York fell [VO] (~1 min 30 s)

`[VISUAL: storm-surge clip; dark skyline stills; a single slow push-in]`

And then, for one of the old world's great cities, it did happen all at once.

New York was the first to go. A century of piecemeal, private sea defences, each built to protect one stretch of waterfront, one development, one owner's interest, met a storm surge that did not care whose wall was whose. In a single week, it was over.

The loss of a city like that does something to everyone who watches it. It turns an abstract forecast into a fact. Within the year, the United Nations had packed up and moved its seat to Paris.

Paris was vulnerable too. But Europe had already begun coordinating its coastal defences, and for a body whose whole purpose was cooperation, it was the only place left that still looked like it believed in the idea.

## Chapter 3 — The withdrawal [VO] (~2 min)

`[VISUAL: empty assembly-hall still; Atlantic coast; the broken American coastline]`

Not everyone made the move.

The delegation representing what remained of the United States withdrew during those first turbulent years in Paris, and it never came back. The official reason was a dispute over binding emissions authority: who had the right to tell whom how much they could burn.

But the less official record tells a different story. A drift was already underway on the other side of the Atlantic before the flooding began: a growing conviction that the crisis was not an engineering problem at all. It was a judgment. And an institution that proposed to manage a judgment, to pump it out, wall it off and ration it, was committing a category error bordering on blasphemy.

Hold on to that idea. It doesn't go away.

There was one exception. The Pacific side kept sending quiet, unofficial observers to the sessions in Paris for decades after the wider withdrawal. And by every account that reached Europe, it was still building walls of its own: on its own terms, under its own authority, to no charter anyone in that chamber had ever signed.

The rest of the withdrawing nation regarded it with suspicion, and with something closer to grief. Like a limb that refused to admit it still belonged to the same body.

## Chapter 4 — How Paris became the Council [VO] (~2 min)

`[VISUAL: Europe/Bulwark explainer footage; mega-city stills; council-chamber imagery]`

What began in Paris as a charter for coordinated flood response became something much larger.

Nations that had spent the previous century treating one another as rivals for the same shrinking resources made an uncomfortable discovery. As the crisis deepened, going it alone was no longer a survival strategy. It was just a slower way of describing failing together.

So they began to pool what they had: money, expertise, and the sheer administrative labour of feeding and relocating populations far too large for any one government to manage. And they did it through the relocated body's own machinery, because by then it was the only machinery still working at the scale the world needed.

No single treaty ever declared that the old nations had stopped being sovereign. Historians have never even agreed which decade it happened in. It just did.

When a formal charter finally came, it created the Unity Government: a properly elected body. A generation later it was restyled the Unified Authority. And by the time the main character of my book, Elara Di Canti, was born, almost nobody called it anything but the Council, its seats still drawn loosely along old national borders that had long since stopped mattering.

`[VISUAL: slow pan across abandoned-coast and wasteland stills; a number counting down]`

Nobody who lived through the transition ever agreed on how much was lost.

The official accounting, pieced together decades later from patchy and contested archives, placed the world's population at its peak at just over ten and a half billion people.

The Unity Government's first formal census could confirm, and house, fewer than six billion within its borders.

That gap is the darkest question in the whole history. Nobody can say how many of those four and a half billion died, and how many simply slipped beyond the reach of the state: into the lawless wastes and the abandoned coastlines, to live a life the government refused to administer, tax, or count.
